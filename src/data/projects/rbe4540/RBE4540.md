## Overview

How do robots pick things up reliably? This course covers the theory behind grasping: how contact forces combine into forces and torques on an object, how to measure whether a grasp is any good, and how cameras close the loop between seeing an object and moving the hand to it. The work is done in **ROS 2**, a **Gazebo** simulation of a robot arm with a palm camera, and **OpenCV**.

## Grasp Taxonomies and Gripper Selection

Human grasps can be classified systematically. The **Feix taxonomy** organizes 33 grasp types by whether they are power, intermediate, or precision grasps, by which parts of the hand oppose each other, and by whether the thumb is abducted or adducted. The same vocabulary helps choose a robot gripper: object size, weight, fragility, and surface properties determine whether a parallel-jaw, suction, or multi-fingered hand fits the task.

## The Grasp Matrix

The grasp matrix $G$ maps the forces applied at each contact to the net wrench (force and torque) on the object:

$$w_o = G\,\lambda, \qquad G = \begin{bmatrix} G_1 & G_2 & \cdots & G_n \end{bmatrix}$$

For contact $i$ at point $c_i$, the moment arm from the object's reference point $o$ is $r_i = c_i - o$. Writing the cross product as a skew-symmetric matrix $S(r_i)$ and letting $R_i$ rotate the contact frame into the object frame, each contact contributes

$$G_i = \begin{bmatrix} R_i & 0 \\ S(r_i)\,R_i & R_i \end{bmatrix} H_i^{\mathsf T}, \qquad S(r) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -r_z & r_y \\ r_z & 0 & -r_x \\ -r_y & r_x & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

where the **contact model** $H_i$ selects which components the contact can transmit:

| Contact model | Transmits | Columns per contact |
|---|---|---|
| Point contact without friction | normal force only | 1 |
| Hard finger (point contact with friction) | 3D force | 3 |
| Soft finger | 3D force + torque about the normal | 4 |

**Example.** For a planar grasp only $F_x$, $F_y$, and $\tau_z$ matter, so the other rows of $G$ are dropped. A finger touching the bottom edge of a 6 × 3 cm block at its midpoint sits at $r = (0, -1.5)$ cm from the center. With its tangent along $x$ and its normal along $y$, it contributes

$$G_{p,1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ -r_y & r_x \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1.5 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

The tangential force produces torque through the 1.5 cm moment arm; the normal force points straight through the center and produces none. Stacking one such block per finger gives the full planar grasp matrix $G_p$.

## Grasp Quality Metrics

If $G$ has full row rank, the contacts can in principle push on the object in every direction (friction limits aside). The singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_d$ of $G$ then measure *how well*:

- **Minimum singular value**, $Q_{\text{MSV}} = \sigma_{\min}(G)$: the grasp's weakest direction; larger means no disturbance direction is poorly resisted
- **Volume of the wrench ellipsoid**, $Q_{\text{VEW}} = \sqrt{\det(G\,G^{\mathsf T})} = \sigma_1\sigma_2\cdots\sigma_d$: overall strength
- **Isotropy**, $Q_{\text{IS}} = \sigma_{\min}/\sigma_{\max}$: how evenly the grasp handles every direction (1 is perfectly uniform)

**Example.** Sliding a fifth finger around the edge of a block already held by four, and recomputing $G$ at every position, shows the metrics can disagree. The minimum singular value and the ellipsoid volume both peak with the new finger at a corner ($\sigma = 5.41, 2.24, 2.23$), where the grasp is strongest overall. Isotropy instead prefers a spot partway along the edge, where the grasp is slightly weaker but far more balanced. Which one is "best" depends on the task.

## Vision for Manipulation

Before a robot can grasp an object it has to find it. From the palm camera's image, classic OpenCV feature detectors pull out the geometry a grasp planner needs:

- **Color thresholding:** keep pixels within a color range, then take the centroid $(\bar u, \bar v) = \frac{1}{N}\sum (u_k, v_k)$ of each blob
- **Canny edges:** image gradients, non-maximum suppression, and two hysteresis thresholds that trade missed edges against noise
- **Harris corners:** score each pixel by $R = \det(M) - k\,\operatorname{tr}(M)^2$, where $M$ is the local gradient structure tensor
- **Hough circles:** every edge pixel votes for the circle centers it could belong to, and the peaks are the detected circles

## Visual Servoing

Visual servoing uses the camera in the control loop: instead of planning a motion once, the robot keeps moving until the image *looks right*. In image-based visual servoing, the features $s$ (here, the four marker centers) are driven to their desired positions $s^*$:

$$e = s - s^*, \qquad \dot s = L_s\,v_c, \qquad v_c = -\lambda\,\hat L_s^{+}\,e$$

The interaction matrix $L_s$ relates camera velocity $v_c$ to how features move in the image. For one point feature at normalized image coordinates $(x, y)$ and depth $Z$:

$$L = \begin{bmatrix} -\frac{1}{Z} & 0 & \frac{x}{Z} & xy & -(1+x^2) & y \\ 0 & -\frac{1}{Z} & \frac{y}{Z} & 1+y^2 & -xy & -x \end{bmatrix}$$

Pixel coordinates are first converted using the camera intrinsics, $x = (u - o_x)\,s_x / f$ and $y = (v - o_y)\,s_y / f$. Stacking four points gives an 8 × 6 matrix, and its pseudoinverse turns image error into a 6-DOF velocity command that brings the gripper to a pre-grasp pose above the object.
