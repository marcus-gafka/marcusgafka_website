## Overview

Fixed solar panels only face the sun directly for a short part of the day. For the final project of this measurement and experimentation course, a partner and I designed and built a **dual-axis solar tracker** that finds the brightest direction and points its panel there, controlled by a Raspberry Pi Pico 2 driving two stepper motors through 3D-printed gearing.

## How It Tracks

- **Find the direction:** the base spins through a full sweep while a ring of three photoresistors, spaced 120° apart, records light levels; the brightest reading (plus each sensor's angular offset) gives the sun's azimuth
- **Find the angle:** at that heading, the panel steps through a set of tilt poses while an ADC reads the panel's voltage, and it settles on the pose that produces the most power
- **Coordinated motion:** both steppers move together on a shared timeline so the panel moves smoothly, and the code picks the shorter of two equivalent rotations to avoid unwinding the wiring

## Design and Validation

<figure class="figure-inline"><img src="/assets/projects/me3902/circuit-diagram.jpg" alt="Hand-drawn circuit diagram of the solar tracker electronics"><figcaption>Early system diagram: sensors, actuators, and the Pico</figcaption></figure>

We scoped the project around measurable goals: at least 20% more power than a fixed panel, pointing within 10° of the best angle, and reacting to a new light direction in under 5 seconds. Each sensor was validated on its own before integration, and the design evolved as we tested: the original plan included an IMU and current sensor, but the photoresistor sweep plus direct voltage feedback proved simpler and more reliable.
